## The idea in one line

**Your voice, to better your life.** In Latin-flavoured English that sentence is the name: *vox* (voice) + *amelior* (to better, as in "ameliorate"). The film argues for the sentence, and in the last shot the sentence turns into the name. The film doesn't spell out the translation; the morph lets the viewer work it out.

## Structure

The film follows the classic five-step persuasive sequence (Monroe's motivated sequence). Each act is one step, and each has a short name that the film says out loud somewhere.

| Act | Step | Time | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 · Your voice | Attention | 0:00–0:10 | A fact of life: we talk all day and forget most of it. |
| 2 · Their rules | Need | 0:10–0:37.5 | The exception: our words online are kept, read and sold on someone else's terms. |
| 3 · Your phone | Satisfaction | 0:37.5–1:12 | The call to action: take your words offline, starting here, and why that's safe. |
| 4 · Your life | Visualization | 1:12–2:41 | A story of what it can do: remember, then help you become better. |
| 5 · The name | Action | 2:41–2:55 | "Your voice, to better your life" becomes "Vox Amelior". Free for Android. |

## Timing

The first cut ran 2:10 and read too fast. This one is paced to be read, using subtitle practice as the floor:

- **Reading speed.** The BBC's subtitle guidance is 160–180 words a minute, about a third of a second a word. A commercial asks for more, because people are also watching the picture, often on a phone. So every line stays up for **about 0.33 s a word plus 1.2 s** after it starts, and a typed answer gets the same once the question has finished typing.
- **Motion that stops.** Words arrive about eight a second and settle in half a second with only a slight blur, so a line is legible almost as soon as it appears. Nothing moves while a line is meant to be read; scenes leave with a short lift.
- **One thing at a time.** Rows, rings and cards arrive one after another, each after the previous one has had its reading time.
- **Typing you can follow.** Questions type at about 30 characters a second, then a short pause before the answer.

Scene start times live in `tools/film/timeline.js`. The picture and the score both read it, so changing a scene's length moves its music with it.

## How the script was written: drafts and reviews

### Draft 1

> You say 16,000 words a day. Most of them, you'll forget. But AI won't. Every chat, every voice note, every question, saved on a server you'll never see. Used to train models. Used to target ads. Your words. Their rules. Take them back. Meet the AI that never leaves your phone. 256-bit encryption. It remembers what Dana said, what to buy, what Milo said. It finds your fallacies. Who starts more fights? You. Vox Amelior.

**Review of draft 1**

- "You say 16,000 words a day" assumes the viewer talks that much. It's an average, so the film says so, and says "we": *"On an average day, we speak about 16,000 words."* "Up to 16,000" would be wrong: 16,000 is the average, and the most talkative people in the study said far more.
- "But AI won't" is a hasty generalization: AI keeps only what you give it. The fix is sharper anyway: *"Except the ones we give to AI. It keeps those."*
- "Every voice note" isn't verified, so it's cut. "Used to train… used to target ads" is true of some services, not all, so the film says what we tell AI *can be*, and every item has a source below.
- "Take them back" promises the impossible: you can't recall what you've already sent. The turn became *"Unless the AI lives on your phone."*
- "Never leaves your phone" is too broad: the app downloads its models. The claim is about your conversations: *"Never sent."*
- "256-bit encryption" on its own implies the app encrypts its data. It doesn't; Android does. So: *"256-bit, by Android."*
- It's a feature list. No one wants anything, and no one changes. It needs a story.
- The name arrives without meaning anything.

### Draft 2

Draft 2 adds the story spine: practical memory, then a child's words, then "ask about yourself", a montage of questions, the fights joke, three months later, and the name built from "your voice, to better your life".

**Review of draft 2**

- "AI has found its way into everything" is an absolute ("everything"), the same habit I flagged in the pitch. The concrete version is stronger: *"AI is in your search. Your inbox. Your photos."*
- The fear section ran long. It's now 23 seconds of facts with "can be", and the turn comes at 0:33.
- The security section was a list. It now has a shape: three layers that close around the phone, from the most technical to the most absolute, then one line that sums them up.
- The self-improvement had no motive. Milo's "I want to be just like you" became the reason: the title turns into *"Worth living up to."*, and the next line turns the app on yourself.
- The fights answer "You, six to five. Jordan ends more of them" was warm but preachy. *"Want to know who apologizes first?" / "Not today."* is funnier and more human.
- The change needed a callback, not a chart: the same question three months later, a different answer.
- "Their rules" needed a payoff, so it gets rewritten on screen: "~~Their~~ Your rules."

### Draft 3 and Pruitt's notes

Draft 3 was the version before this one. Pruitt's notes on it, and what changed:

- **"Except the ones we give to AI" was too narrow.** It became *"Except our words online."*: everything we search, type and watch, which the screen now fills with. AI comes in a beat later ("Now AI is in your search…"), as the latest place our words go.
- **The third line should be about selling.** "Used for ads we'll definitely see" became *"sold to companies we've never heard of."* The rhythm stays (never see, never meet, never heard of) and it ends on the strongest fact.
- **The turn should be a call to action: taking it offline is the move.** "Unless the AI lives on your phone" became *"Take your words offline. Start here."*, and the phone rises on "here".
- **The name should explain itself.** The end card no longer translates *vox* and *amelior*; the morph from "Your voice, to better your life" does that work.
- **A more everyday memory.** Dana's reset steps became *"What did Jordan say I shouldn't forget?"* (plumber, cake, Mom's birthday), which also sets up "Call Mom back" and "And you called Mom. Every Sunday."
- **Kept as it was:** "Our words. Their rules." and its rewrite, "Your words. Your phone. Your rules."

### Draft 4 and the last notes

- **Too fast to read.** The film was retimed to the reading-speed rules in [Timing](#timing). It now runs 2:55.
- **"Which words do I use wrong?"** became *"Which words do I use incorrectly?"*, and the example is a high-level word that's easy to misuse: "That plan is **ingenuous**." *Ingenuous* means naive; the speaker meant *ingenious* (clever). Merriam-Webster draws exactly this line.
- Two answers were shortened so they can be read in time: "Thursday's pitch. You'd rehearsed it out loud the night before." and "What thinking traps do I fall into?" (the card's source line, "Everything · 3,412 lines", shows it looked through everything).

### Final

The final is the script above. The last pass was for phone legibility: every line fits a 4:5 frame at a size that reads at 360px wide, and no headline breaks into orphans.

## Rhetoric

| Line | Device |
| --- | --- |
| "On an average day, we speak about 16,000 words." | A statistic as the hook (logos); the inclusive "we" |
| "Most of them slip away. / Except our words online. Those, they keep." | Antithesis and reversal: what we lose vs what is kept from us; "they" stays unnamed on purpose |
| The flood of searches, messages and videos | Accumulation, shown rather than said |
| "Now AI is in your search. Your inbox. Your photos." | Anaphora, tricolon, asyndeton |
| "stored on servers we'll never see, / read by people we'll never meet, / sold to companies we've never heard of." | Tricolon with parallel endings (never see, never meet, never heard of) and a climax from storing to selling |
| "Our words. Their rules." | Antithesis, parallelism, ellipsis |
| "Take your words offline. / Start here." | The turn (peripeteia) as a call to action: two imperatives, the second pointing at the phone |
| "It listens / thinks / remembers *on your phone.*" | Epistrophe, tricolon, climax |
| "Encrypted. Sealed. Never sent." | Asyndeton, tricolon, climax from technical to absolute |
| "You can't leak what you never send." | Epigram |
| "Your words. Your phone. ~~Their~~ Your rules." | Callback that reverses Act 2, with anaphora |
| "Now let your words work for you." | Reversal: the words that worked for them work for you |
| "Worth living up to." | Reframing: a memory becomes a motive |
| "So ask about the voice you hear most. / Yours." | Hypophora (a question, then the answer) with a reveal |
| The montage | Accumulation: the questions literally pile up |
| "You. Six to five." / "Want to know who apologizes first?" / "Not today." | Comic rule of three: setup, beat, button |
| "Who starts more of our fights now?" / "Hard to say. There haven't been many." | Callback and understatement |
| "And you called Mom. Every Sunday." | Resolution of "Call Mom back." |
| "Your voice, to better your life." → "Vox Amelior" | Etymology as the reveal: the promise turns into the name, and the viewer translates it themselves |

## Claims and how they were checked

Every claim in the film was checked against the app's code or a published source. "Code" means `vox_mobile/` on branch `claude/laughing-cerf-zer2dj` (631d215) of Vox_Amelior.

| Claim | Basis |
| --- | --- |
| About 16,000 words a day, on average | Mehl et al., *Science* 317 (2007): about 16,000 for both women and men, with a wide range between people |
| Most of them slip away | Brown-Schmidt & Benjamin (2018): after a short delay, people may recall fewer than 20% of the ideas in a conversation |
| Our words online can be stored on servers | Search engines, apps and chat assistants keep history on their servers by default |
| …read by people | In 2019, contractors were found listening to Siri, Alexa and Google Assistant recordings; Google's Gemini privacy notice says human reviewers read some conversations, kept up to three years |
| …sold to companies we've never heard of | The FTC found that Avast, through Jumpshot, sold browsing data to more than 100 companies (2014–2020). Its data-broker study describes companies that buy and sell consumer data without consumers' knowledge. Meta also uses Meta AI chats to target ads (since December 16, 2025) |
| It listens, thinks and remembers on your phone | Speech (sherpa-onnx), the language model (Gemma via LiteRT) and search all run on the phone |
| Works in airplane mode | The only network code is the model downloader (`models/resumable_downloader.dart`) and webhooks you set up (`automation/http_sender.dart`). Checked in the code, not yet on a phone in airplane mode |
| Encrypted: 256-bit, by Android | File-based encryption is required on phones launched with Android 10 or later; the default is AES-256-XTS (Adiantum on low-end phones, also with 256-bit keys) |
| Keyed to your passcode | Apps store data in credential-encrypted storage by default, which "is only available after the user has unlocked the device" |
| Sealed: other apps can't read it | The database (`vox.db`) and voice clips live in the app's private storage (`getApplicationSupportDirectory`), which Android's app sandbox keeps from other apps |
| No cloud backups | `android:allowBackup="false"` in the app's manifest turns off cloud backups |
| Never sent; no server to breach | The app has no server, no analytics and no cloud AI. Downloaded models are checked against a SHA-256 fingerprint before use. Webhooks are off unless you set them up, and can be signed with HMAC-SHA256 |
| The questions about yourself | Reviews run on the phone. Built-in ones include Logical fallacies, Fights & tension, Kind words and Promises & to-dos, and "Your own" takes any question. "Go through everything" reads a whole period in parts (`assistant/review_templates.dart`, `assistant/review_engine.dart`) |
| Heated moments, laughter, vs. spring | Insights counts words, laughter, moods and heated moments, and compares them with the period before (`data/insights_repository.dart`) |
| The name | *Vox* is Latin for "voice". "Ameliorate" means "to make better", via French *améliorer* from Latin *melior*, "better". "Amelior" is a coinage from it, so the film says "as in 'ameliorate'" |

What the film deliberately doesn't claim:

- **The app's own encryption.** The app doesn't add an encryption layer of its own; its data is protected by Android's. Encrypting the database itself (SQLCipher, with the key in Android's Keystore) would make "encrypted twice" true; that would be a change to the app, not the film.
- **Correct answers.** The people, conversations and answers are examples, and answers from a small on-device model can be wrong. The fine print says so.
- **Lexile scores.** "Which day did I sound sharpest?" stands in for a reading-level score. Lexile is a trademark, and the app doesn't compute one.

## Sound

The film has no voice-over. Synthetic voices tend to sound synthetic, and the on-screen text already carries every line. Instead there's a quiet score, generated by `tools/film/score.mjs`, with nothing recorded and nothing licensed:

- **Bed:** a slow, soft pad in D major. Act 2 turns cooler (B minor, G with a raised 4th) over a slow heartbeat. After "Their rules" the music falls away to near silence for a beat, then comes back at "Unless".
- **Piano:** a felt-piano line marks the key moments: a rising line as the number counts, falling notes as words slip away, a warm chord at "Worth living up to".
- **Act 4:** a quiet pulse that stops dead for "You. Six to five." and comes back with a two-note wink at "Not today."
- **The name:** a rising run of bells as the words turn into "Vox Amelior", and one chord as the mark lands.
- **Effects:** soft typing for each question, a soft latch as each security ring closes, a whoosh as each card arrives.
- **Level:** normalised to −17 LUFS, a calm level close to other web video.

If you'd like a voice, your own voice reading the on-screen lines would suit a film about your voice; `score.mjs` can mix it in.

## How it's made

1. `node tools/film/bake.mjs` renders the 3D stills with Three.js: the voice field and the phone with three real app screens.
2. `film.html` moves them in CSS 3D and animates the type word by word. Each frame is a pure function of time; `?format=portrait` switches to the phone layout.
3. `node tools/film/score.mjs score.wav` writes the soundtrack.
4. `render.mjs` captures 5,250 frames at 30 fps per cut, then encodes an H.264 MP4 with AAC audio and a WebP poster:

   ```
   node tools/promo-video/render.mjs --page tools/film/film.html --name vox-film --audio score.wav
   node tools/promo-video/render.mjs --page "tools/film/film.html?format=portrait" --name vox-film-portrait --audio score.wav
   ```
